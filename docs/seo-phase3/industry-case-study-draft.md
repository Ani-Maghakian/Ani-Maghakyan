# Designing a 200+ Episode Drama: Lessons from Elen’s Diary

**Editorial draft for international and diaspora producers — author review required before publication.**

This draft separates verifiable credits from a proposed production workflow. The workflow below must be checked against Ani’s actual practice before it is presented as a retrospective case study. It does not claim a particular writers’ room size, budget, delivery speed, audience result or production efficiency.

## The producer’s challenge

A long-running drama must sustain emotional investment while giving a production team clear decisions to execute. A compelling pilot is only the beginning. Producers also need a repeatable story engine, continuity across many episodes, and a practical process for handling changes without losing the series’ identity.

Elen’s Diary offers a concrete scale reference: Ani Maghakyan’s author filmography records two seasons and 421 episodes, divided into 197 and 224 episodes. Her documented involvement includes screenwriting and the original concept. These figures describe the work’s scale; they do not establish that she independently wrote every episode or managed a staffed writers’ room.

## From concept to a durable story engine

For a producer commissioning a long-running drama, development should answer three questions before scripts accumulate: what keeps the characters in meaningful conflict, what changes irreversibly as the story progresses, and what can generate fresh situations without repeating the same emotional beat?

A useful development package would contain a series premise, character objectives and contradictions, relationship maps, season turning points and a sample episode. For diaspora productions, it should also define the intended audience, language choices and the cultural details the story must preserve. A local setting can carry universal stakes when the characters’ choices remain specific.

## Building the episode architecture

A proposed workflow starts with the season destination and works back toward episode decisions. A season map tracks the main relationship and major reversals. Shorter blocks translate those milestones into episode objectives. Each episode outline identifies the character driving the action, the choice under pressure, and the consequence that changes the next episode.

Parallel storylines should have different jobs. One can advance the central conflict, another reveal a character’s private cost, and a third alter an alliance. This gives an episode variety while keeping the series focused. A cliffhanger should follow from a meaningful choice or discovery rather than exist only to delay an answer.

## Keeping the scripts usable in production

A continuity record can track chronology, what each character knows, relationship changes and unresolved promises. A decision log can distinguish an approved story change from an idea still under discussion. Those two records help producers and writers understand what a requested revision affects.

Before dialogue is locked, a production review should test the outline against available cast, locations and the agreed scope. When a production constraint changes, the first question is which dramatic purpose the scene serves. That purpose can guide a rewrite while preserving the character’s decision and the episode’s consequence.

## Choosing the right engagement

**Series Development:** define the concept, story engine, characters, season map and pilot materials before committing to a longer script order.

**Screenwriting:** agree the format, episode scope, source materials, revision rounds and approval responsibilities, then commission the required scripts.

**Showrunning:** where this service is commissioned, define creative decision authority, coordination with the producer and director, continuity responsibilities and the production handoff. A screenwriting credit alone should not be presented as proof of showrunning on a specific project.

## A practical first conversation

For international and diaspora producers, the initial discussion can begin with the intended platform, audience, language, format, target episode order and development stage. There is no need to submit an unpublished script or confidential concept in the first message. The next step is to agree a suitable development scope and a process for sharing materials.
